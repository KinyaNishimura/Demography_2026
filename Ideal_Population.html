The Ideal Population

We consider that the properties of an individual are characterized by its age. The parameters of survival and reproduction are taken as the characteristics of each age. s0,s1,⋯ are the age-specific survival rates, and b0,b1,⋯ are the age-specific fertility rates. Here, only females are counted, and bx is the average number of female offspring produced by a female of age x.

We call a population an ideal population if it consists of individuals whose survival and fertility rates are constant over time and are not affected by external events (such as environmental fluctuations or the strength of interactions among individuals) or by differences in the internal traits of individuals; that is, a population that reflects only the standard, constant properties of its individuals.

We analyze the properties of the ideal population.

Note: This matrix is for the case in which individuals are counted just after reproduction (post-breeding census). Individuals of age 0 are those just born at that time. A fraction sj of individuals of age j survive to become age j+1 at the next time step, and there they produce bj+1 offspring. Therefore, the elements of the first row are sjbj+1.

Eq. (1) can be solved in the following form:

(2)nt=g1λ1t(|u1|)+g2λ2t(|u2|)+⋯+gmλmt(|um|)

Here, λ1,λ2,⋯,λm are the m eigenvalues of the matrix L, and u1,u2,⋯,um are the eigenvectors (column vectors) corresponding to each eigenvalue. g1,g2,⋯,gm are constants determined by the initial age structure n0.

For the Leslie matrix of a human population, λ1 is a positive real number whose absolute value is larger than that of any other eigenvalue ( λ1>|λ2|≥|λ3|≥⋯). λ1 and u1 are called the dominant eigenvalue and the dominant eigenvector, respectively.

The right-hand side of Eq. (2) can be divided into two parts as follows:

nt=g1λ1t(|u1|)⏟①+g2λ2t(|u2|)+⋯+gmλmt(|um|)⏟②.

A population whose age structure has been shaped by some events passes through a transient state in which ② is at work, and then comes to show consistent properties dominated by ①. Because the absolute value of λ1 is larger than that of the other eigenvalues, ② becomes negligibly small compared with ① as time passes. The following expresses these properties (hereafter, we write λ=λ1 and u=u1):

nt∝λt(|u|)=λt(u0u1⋮)∝λt(n~0n~1⋮)(t→∞).

The dominant eigenvalue λ of the matrix L represents the consistent per-period rate of change in the total population size, and the corresponding dominant eigenvector u represents the consistent composition of the numbers of individuals by age within the population.

Note: The per-period rate of change λ here is the ratio of the total size at the next time step to the current total size, λ=Nt+1/Nt (where Nt is the total size at time t). If λ>1, the population increases; if λ<1, it decreases. If λ=1, the total size does not change.

The dominant eigenvector u can be expressed as follows:

(3)u∝(l0l1λ−1l2λ−2l3λ−3⋮),lx=s0s1⋯sx−1,  l0=1.

The sequence of elements of the vector on the right-hand side can be interpreted as the stable age distribution, with the first element set to 1 (=l0). When plotted as a graph, it visualizes the shape of the age composition of the population at any time. In what follows, we focus on the properties of ①, the basic framework of the ideal population.

 

The characteristics of a population arise from the nature of the individual lives that comprise it.

The following equation concisely expresses the properties of population change in the ideal population:

(4)nt∝λt(l0l1λ−1l2λ−2l3λ−3⋮).

  • The total size of the population changes at a constant per-period rate of change λ.

  • The population has a consistent composition of the numbers of individuals by age. This composition is determined by the survival schedule lx and the per-period rate of change λ.

In addition, the following important points should be noted:

  • The survival schedule lx is determined by the age-specific survival rates s0,s1,⋯ of the individuals that make up the population.

  • The per-period rate of change λ is determined by the age-specific survival rates s0,s1,⋯ and the age-specific fertility rates b0,b1,⋯ of the individuals that make up the population.

 

The traits of individuals determine the nature of the population.

We have seen that, in the ideal population, there is a mechanism by which the life-history parameters of individuals (s0,s1,⋯,b0,b1,b2,⋯) determine the structure of the population (the stable age structure). Two further mechanisms are explained below.

▪️Reproductive Contribution of Each Age Group

In the ideal population, we can formally express the probability of the age of the mother that produced a given newborn. When one newborn is chosen, the probabilities that its mother was of age 1, age 2, age 3, … are given, in order, by the elements of the following vector:

(5)(b1l1λ−1, b2l2λ−2, b3l3λ−3, ⋯).

From this, the following equation holds:

(6)1=b1l1λ−1+b2l2λ−2+b3l3λ−3+⋯

Each term on the right-hand side of Eq. (6) is the probability that the mother of a newborn is of the corresponding age, and these probabilities sum to 1. Each term on the right-hand side also has the meaning of "the fraction, when reproduction occurs and a cohort of newborns is born, that was produced by individuals of each age." Eq. (6) is called the Euler–Lotka equation.

Let the column vector of age-specific fertility rates be

(7)b=(b0b1b2b3⋮),  where b0=0.

If the dominant eigenvector u of the Leslie matrix is taken as the vector on the right-hand side of Eq. (3), with the first element set to 1, then Eq. (6) is written as

(8)1=uTb=(l0l1λ−1l2λ−2l3λ−3⋯)(b0b1b2b3⋮).

From the meaning of Eq. (8), we see that which age groups the reproduction comes from (the reproductive contribution of each age group) can be evaluated by the product uxbx of the relative frequency ux of each age in the population and the age-specific fertility rate bx.

 

▪️The concept of Reproductive value

Let us consider "the contribution of an individual of each age to population growth." This is formalized by the concept of the reproductive value of an individual of each age. It is necessary to make clear how this differs from the concept of "the reproductive contribution of each age group." The differences between these concepts are as follows.

The reproductive value is the relative contribution of one individual of each age to the increase in population size over its subsequent ages (the value relative to that of a newborn, v0=1), and is expressed by the following equation:

(9)RVx=vx=λxlx∑i=x+1biliλ−i.

Note: In a post-breeding census, an individual counted as age x has already produced its offspring bx at that age (those offspring are counted as age 0 at the same time). Therefore, the contribution this individual will make to the population from now on starts at age x+1, and the sum begins at i=x+1. In this case, vx is the component of the left eigenvector of the Leslie matrix L (Eq. (1)) corresponding to the dominant eigenvalue λ.

For x=0, since b0=0, we have v0=∑i=1biliλ−i=1, which agrees with the Euler–Lotka equation (Eq. (6)).

In Fisher's classical definition, the sum begins at i=x (the value of an individual that has just reached age x, before reproducing at that age). In that case, for x≥1, the value differs from vx here by bx.
